# Document playbooks — business-document genres

The genre layer for documents: what a proposal, an executive brief, a press release, or a case study actually IS, and the two regulated genres that carry a mandatory disclaimer. Page craft — grid, type ladder, density, vertical balance — lives in references/document-design.md; these decide WHAT the pages say. Settle the arc before designing to it: a document laid out beautifully against the wrong arc is still the wrong document.

Research for these genres runs on `moda web search` / `moda web read` (references/web.md); each playbook says below how much research it actually warrants, and the answer is often none.

---

## Proposal

Triggers: proposal, pitch, partnership proposal, project proposal, business proposal, business case, pitch document, RFP response, SOW, scope of work, internal proposal, budget request, funding request, investment proposal, sponsorship proposal.

### Objective

A persuasive document that moves the reader from "interesting" to "approved": problem → solution → cost → return. Not a neutral analysis. 1,500-2,500 words — longer than that is a SOW; push detail into appendices.

### Document structure

1. **Title header** — `[Project Name]` — Proposal for `[Recipient]`, prepared by `[Author/Team]`, date, optional one-line value proposition.
2. **Executive summary** — one paragraph, 4-6 sentences: problem → solution → key benefit → investment → expected outcome. Model: "`[Company]` is losing an estimated `[$X/month]` to `[problem]`. This proposal outlines a `[duration]` engagement to `[solution]`, with an expected return of `[outcome]`. Total investment: `[$amount]`. We recommend starting `[timeline]`."
3. **Problem statement** — 2-3 paragraphs quantifying the pain (`[X hours/week]`, `[$Y]` at risk). No solution yet.
4. **Proposed solution** — approach overview (1 paragraph, including why this over alternatives); 3-5 key components or workstreams as bullets or a table, each with a name, 1-2 sentences, and what it produces; why this approach, referencing relevant experience. Sell the outcome, not the implementation spec.
5. **Timeline and milestones** — total duration, decision points, and dependencies on client inputs.

   | Phase | Duration | Deliverables |
   |-------|----------|-------------|
   | `[Phase 1: Discovery]` | `[2 weeks]` | `[Deliverable A, Deliverable B]` |
   | `[Phase 2: Implementation]` | `[6 weeks]` | `[Deliverable C, Deliverable D]` |
   | `[Phase 3: Launch + Support]` | `[2 weeks]` | `[Deliverable E, go-live]` |

6. **Investment** (call it that, not "cost") — total for `[scope summary]`, breakdown by phase or workstream, explicit what's included *and excluded*, payment terms (`[50% upfront, 50% on completion]`).
7. **Expected outcomes / ROI** — 3 quantified results (`[reduce processing time from 4 hours to 45 minutes]`, `[$200K annual savings]`). Before/after and `[$X investment]` → `[$Y return]` framing where the numbers exist; where the return is qualitative, say so rather than inventing numbers.
8. **Why us** — 1 paragraph + 3-4 proof points of *relevant* delivered work, not a company bio.
9. **Next steps** — 2-3 sentences naming the exact action, owner, and date: "To proceed, we'd need `[signed agreement]` by `[date]`. `[Name]` will follow up on `[date]`."

### Brand kit usage

When a brand kit is present:

- **Tone** shapes the warmth/formality balance. "Dynamic + professional" → confident forward-looking language. "Supportive + approachable" → collaborative framing.
- **Values** inform what to emphasize in qualifications and approach. Values "transparency" → be explicit about assumptions and limitations.
- **Aesthetic** informs formatting density. "Minimal" → clean tables and whitespace. "Detailed" → fuller narrative per section.

### Placeholders

Bracket what is unknown and make every placeholder show the *shape* of what belongs there — `[Client Name, e.g., Meridian Logistics]`, `[Investment amount, e.g., $85,000]`, `[Expected outcome, e.g., 60% reduction in manual reporting hours]`, `[Qualification, e.g., delivered similar migrations for 3 Fortune 500 logistics companies]`. Details the user already gave are not placeholders; use them directly.

### Research

Search only for industry benchmarks that validate the problem size, competitor pricing for context, or case studies of similar solutions. Skip search entirely when the proposal is internal, the user supplied the specifics, or the content is about the user's own offering.

## Executive brief

Triggers: executive summary, brief, one-pager, executive brief, decision memo, memo, board update, leadership update, stakeholder update, decision brief, executive overview, management summary, steering committee update.

### Objective

A document a busy executive reads in 2-3 minutes and comes away knowing the situation, the options, and the recommended action. Brevity is the defining constraint: under 2 pages, ~800 words max. Not a compressed research report — a distinct format built for decision speed.

### Document structure

1. **Title line** — `[Topic] — [Document type]`, e.g. "Q3 Infrastructure Migration — Decision Memo."
2. **TL;DR / Recommendation** — 2-4 bullets leading with the recommendation, not the context: "We recommend proceeding with Vendor A ($180K/yr). They scored highest on integration speed and compliance coverage. Deployment can begin in Q2."
3. **Context** — 3-4 sentences maximum, and only what the reader needs to evaluate the recommendation, not background they already have.
4. **Analysis** — structured, not narrative; bullets and tables over prose. Options evaluation → comparison table on 3-5 criteria plus 1-2 sentences per option interpreting (not narrating) the tradeoff. Status update → metrics or milestones as bullets with status indicators, flagging what needs attention. Problem analysis → root cause → impact → proposed fix.
5. **Risks and mitigations** — 3-5 bullets, each risk → likelihood/severity → mitigation.
6. **The Ask** — 2-3 sentences: approval, budget, a decision between options, or feedback by a date. If there is no ask, state next steps and who owns them.

Every claim carries a specific number, date, or name — "$180K annually," not "a significant investment." Include only what differentiates the options; equal depth on everything wastes the page.

### Brand kit usage

Executive briefs are internal-facing, so the brand kit matters less than in marketing content. When present, **tone** applies lightly (clarity always wins over warmth here) and **values** can inform emphasis — values "transparency" → surface more risks explicitly.

### Placeholders

Bracket what is unknown and make every placeholder show the *shape* of what belongs there — `[Recommendation, e.g., proceed with Vendor A at $180K/yr]`, `[Metric, e.g., 94% uptime over the trailing 90 days]`, `[Owner, e.g., VP Engineering owns migration execution]`. Details the user already gave are not placeholders; use them directly.

### Research

Search only for pricing benchmarks, competitor approaches, or industry standards that strengthen the recommendation ("industry average is X, our target is Y"). Skip search for internal decisions, status updates, or when the prompt already carries the relevant data.

## Press release

Triggers: press release, PR, announcement, media release, news release, company announcement, product launch announcement, partnership announcement, funding announcement, press statement, media advisory.

### Objective

A news document a journalist could run with minimal editing: rigid inverted pyramid, factual and quotable, third person throughout except inside quotes. 400-800 words.

### Document structure

1. **Headline** — one line, present tense, no period, stating the news itself: "`[Company]` Launches `[Product]` to `[solve specific problem for specific audience]`" or "`[Company]` Raises `[$X]` Series B to Expand `[capability]`".
2. **Subheadline** (optional) — the key supporting detail the headline couldn't fit, usually a number, customer name, or availability date: "New platform reduces fleet charging costs by up to 40%; available to enterprise customers starting `[date]`".
3. **Dateline + lead paragraph** — format **`[CITY, STATE]` — `[Month Day, Year]`** —, then 3-4 sentences answering who, what, when, where, why. Model: "`[Company]`, a `[one-line description]`, today announced `[the news]`. The `[product]` enables `[audience]` to `[key benefit]`. `[Supporting context — market size, customer demand, or the problem solved]`. `[Product]` is available `[date / immediately / in beta]`."
4. **Supporting body** — 2-3 paragraphs in descending importance: (2) how it works or what it includes — for funding, use of proceeds; for a partnership, what each party brings; (3) market context or customer traction, with a real industry figure where research supports one; (4) availability, pricing, geographic rollout if needed.
5. **Executive quote** — 2-3 sentences attributed to a named executive with title, adding vision or "why now" that factual prose can't carry: "`[e.g., 'Fleet operators have been flying blind on charging costs. [Product] gives them the visibility to turn EV charging from a cost center into a competitive advantage.']`" said `[Name]`, `[Title]` of `[Company]`. Add a customer or partner quote about real impact when available.
6. **About `[Company]`** — boilerplate, 3-4 sentences, reusable across releases: "`[Company]` is a `[category]` company that helps `[audience]` `[do what]`. Founded in `[year]`, the company serves `[X customers / notable names]` and has raised `[$Y]` from `[investors]`. Learn more at `[website URL]`."
7. **Media contact**

   ```
   Media Contact:
   [Name]
   [Title], [Company]
   [email]
   [phone — optional]
   ```

### Brand kit usage

Press releases are formulaic, so brand kit influence is lighter than in other content:

- **Tone** applies mainly inside quotes — a "dynamic" brand's exec quote is more forward-looking; a "warm" brand's is more customer-centric.
- **Values** inform which proof points to emphasize in the body and boilerplate.
- **Boilerplate** should reuse the brand's own self-description if the kit has one.

### Placeholders

Bracket what is unknown and make every placeholder show the *shape* of what belongs there — `[Company Name, e.g., Flipturn, Inc.]`, `[City, State, e.g., San Francisco, CA]`, `[Executive Name and Title, e.g., Sarah Chen, CEO]`, `[Key metric, e.g., serving 200+ enterprise fleets across North America]`. Details the user already gave are not placeholders; use them directly.

### Research

Search only for market size or trend data that contextualizes the announcement, competitor positioning, or recent related announcements. Skip search when the user supplied the facts or the announcement is a straightforward launch or funding round.

## Case study

Triggers: case study, customer story, success story, customer spotlight, client case study, customer testimonial, client success, customer case, customer win, customer highlight, customer example.

### Objective

Marketing collateral, not a research report: a narrative a prospect reads and thinks "I want those results." Persuade through specificity and story, not exhaustive analysis. 500–1,500 words.

### Document structure

1. **Headline** — lead with the result, not the customer name: `[Customer/Industry]` + `[specific result]` + with `[Product/Platform]`.
2. **Snapshot box** — Customer (name + one-line description), Industry, Challenge, Key result, Timeline. A skimmer should get the whole story from this block alone.
3. **The Challenge** (1–2 paragraphs) — open on the pain, not on background. Quantify the consequence.
4. **The Solution** (1–2 paragraphs) — what changed day-to-day, and how fast. The customer is the hero; the product is what they used.
5. **The Results** — 3–5 quantified metrics as a table or stat callouts, each with one line of context. Before/after framing where available.
6. **Hero quote** (1–2 sentences) — names a specific outcome and what it meant. Attributed to a named person with title.
7. **What's next / CTA** (2–3 sentences).

### Brand kit usage

When a brand kit is present:

- **Tone** maps to sentence rhythm and word choice. "Professional + dynamic" → punchy active verbs. "Warm + approachable" → conversational transitions, first-person customer voice.
- **Values** map to which results lead. Values "reliability" → lead with uptime or consistency. Values "innovation" → lead with what is novel about the approach.
- **Aesthetic** maps to formatting density. "Minimal/sleek" → stat callouts and whitespace. "Detailed/thorough" → fuller narrative paragraphs.

### Placeholders

Bracket what is unknown, and make every placeholder show the *shape* of what belongs there — `[Customer Name, e.g., Meridian Logistics]`, `[specific metric, e.g., reduced average charge time by 34%]`, `[Title, e.g., VP of Operations]`. Details the user already gave are not placeholders; use them directly.

### Research

Search only for what the draft cannot supply: industry benchmarks that contextualize a result, or competitor context behind a switching story. If the user gave enough to write a compelling draft, write it — research must not block the draft.

## Regulated genres — the two mandatory disclaimers

Two document types carry a compliance-shaped obligation. The disclaimer is not a footer and not optional: it goes at the TOP of the document, on the first page, before the summary, and it survives every export. Design it as a bordered block, not gray 8pt type at the bottom.

## Financial analysis

Triggers: investment research, stock analysis, financial analysis, should I invest, investment opportunity, financial overview, stock research, valuation, bull case bear case, investment thesis, financial research, invest in, portfolio, stock, shares, buy or rent, index funds, retirement, 401k, financial planning.

### Objective

Help the user make an informed financial decision by synthesizing current data and expert analysis. The reader should finish understanding the key factors, risks, and trade-offs — not be told what to do.

**IMPORTANT: Every document produced under this skill MUST include a prominent disclaimer at the top: "This is informational research only, not financial advice. Consult a qualified financial advisor before making investment decisions." This is non-negotiable.**

### Classify the request

- **Investment/market analysis** (stock, asset, sector): output is a bull/bear thesis, never a buy/sell recommendation.
- **Personal finance decisions** (buy vs. rent, index funds vs. stocks, retirement, debt payoff): output is a framework calibrated to the user's situation, not just market data.

### Phase 1: Research

#### Never state a financial figure from memory

Your training data is months out of date; a stock may have moved 30%, rates may have been cut, an earnings report may have changed the picture. Every number — price, P/E, interest rate, home price, fund return — must come from a current web search with a cited source and a date. Check article dates: an analysis from three months ago uses different numbers, and a rates article from before the last Fed meeting is stale.

#### Retrieve macro context early

- `"federal reserve interest rate [current month] [year]"`
- `"S&P 500 [year]"`, `"market outlook [year]"`
- `"[sector] outlook [year]"` — e.g. "housing market 2026"
- `"inflation rate [current month] [year]"`

A stock at 15x earnings looks different at 3% rates vs. 6%; a buy-vs-rent answer depends entirely on local mortgage rates and supply. Connect the specific analysis back to this backdrop rather than analyzing in isolation.

#### Search strategy by type

**Investment/stock analysis:**
- `"[company] earnings [latest quarter]"`, `"[ticker] financials"`
- `"[company] stock analysis [year]"`, `"[ticker] bull case bear case"`, `"[ticker] price target"`
- `"[company] competitors"`, `"[company] market share"`, `"[sector] competitive landscape"`
- `"[company] risks"`, `"[company] challenges [year]"`
- `"[company] 10-K"`, `"[company] annual report"` (deep dives on public companies)

**Personal finance decisions:**
- `"mortgage rates [month] [year]"`, `"[index fund] returns [year]"`, `"[city] housing market [year]"`
- `"buy vs rent calculator [year]"`, `"[decision] pros cons [year]"`
- `"[city] median home price [year]"`, `"[401k/IRA] contribution limits [year]"`, `"[tax bracket] [year]"`

#### Source priority for reading

1. Financial data providers — Yahoo Finance, Google Finance, Morningstar, Seeking Alpha
2. Earnings releases and SEC filings — investor relations pages, SEC.gov
3. Major financial outlets — Bloomberg, CNBC, WSJ, Reuters
4. Bank/asset-manager research — J.P. Morgan, Goldman Sachs, Morningstar
5. Official government sources — Federal Reserve, BLS, Census Bureau
6. Personal finance outlets — Investopedia, NerdWallet, Bankrate

### Phase 2: Writing

#### Document structure

**Investment/stock analysis:**

1. **Disclaimer** — the mandatory financial disclaimer block above, at the top of page one before anything else.
2. **Summary & Key Takeaway** — 3-5 bullets: current price, thesis in one sentence, primary risk in one sentence. End with "This is informational research, not financial advice."
3. **How to Think About [This Investment]** — the framework: which metrics drive this asset's value and why, which macro factors matter, what to watch. The most durable section — the numbers go stale, this doesn't. Calibrate to the user's apparent sophistication.
4. **Macro Context** — 1-2 short paragraphs, cited and dated.
5. **Company / Asset Overview** — business model, market position, current financials with cited numbers.
6. **Bull Case** — 3-5 reasons with specific evidence. Not "the company is growing" but "revenue grew 62% YoY to $57B in Q3 FY2026, driven by data center demand [Source]."
7. **Bear Case** — 3-5 risks at the same evidence standard: valuation, competitive, macro, execution.
8. **Valuation Context** — current multiples (P/E, P/S, EV/EBITDA as relevant) vs. history and peers. Not a target price — target prices imply false precision; avoid them and specific return predictions entirely.
9. **Key Risks** — concrete and distinct from the bear case: what could go wrong that isn't priced in?
10. **Sources**.

**Personal finance decisions:**

1. **Disclaimer** — same mandatory block, top of page one.
2. **Summary & Recommendation Framework** — situational: "X makes sense if [condition A], Y if [condition B]," not "do X."
3. **How to Think About [This Decision]** — the underlying economics, not a pros/cons list. For buy vs. rent: opportunity cost of the down payment, mortgage rate vs. investment returns, time horizon, local market dynamics, tax treatment.
4. **Current Market Conditions** — rates, prices, returns, tax rules, cited and dated.
5. **Analysis** — walk the decision through the framework with the current numbers; show the math.
6. **Key Factors for Your Situation** — what to weight most given their stated context.
7. **What to Watch** — "if rates drop below X%, the math shifts toward buying."
8. **Sources**.

Always present bull AND bear, and label fact vs. opinion: "revenue grew 62% YoY" is fact; "this growth is sustainable" is opinion.

#### Citations

Every figure cites its source with an inline markdown link, with publication date where visible. **Sources** section lists all URLs. When figures conflict across sources (common), note the discrepancy and say which source is more authoritative.

### Scope calibration

- **Quick take** (single stock or simple question): disclaimer first (mandatory, every scope) + summary + macro + bull/bear + key risks + sources.
- **Standard analysis**: full structure, multiple search rounds.
- **Deep dive**: full structure with expanded macro, competitive landscape, peer valuation comparison, and historical context.

## Regulatory research

Triggers: compliance, regulatory, regulation, GDPR, SOC 2, HIPAA, FDA, PCI DSS, CCPA, privacy law, data protection, compliance requirements, regulatory requirements, what are the requirements, do I need to comply, certification, audit, legal requirements, licensing requirements, permit, zoning.

### Objective

Translate dense regulatory language into a clear, actionable guide: what applies to the user, what it means in practice, and what to do about it.

**Mandatory**: every document produced with this skill MUST open with a disclaimer that this is informational research, not legal or professional compliance advice.

### Phase 1: Research

Identify which kind of question this is, since it sets what the user needs: **compliance standard** (SOC 2, ISO 27001, PCI DSS — what's required, timeline, cost), **data privacy law** (GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, HIPAA — applicability, obligations, penalties), **industry regulation** (FDA, financial licensing, food safety — process and steps), or **licensing/permits** (where to apply, cost, duration).

Run diverse queries — one `moda web search` per query, not one query rephrased. Mix these patterns:

- Official sources first: `"[regulation]" site:gov`, `"[regulation]" site:europa.eu`, `"[standard] requirements" site:[official body]` — e.g. `"510(k)" site:fda.gov`
- Practical guides: `"[regulation] requirements [user's context]"` — e.g. "GDPR requirements SaaS company"
- Checklists: `"[regulation] compliance checklist [year]"`
- Process: `"[regulation] process steps"`, `"[regulation] timeline how long"`
- Recent changes: `"[regulation] changes [year]"`, `"new [regulation] requirements"`
- Cost: `"[regulation] cost"`, `"[regulation] for small business"`

At least two rounds: one broad (what it is, whether it applies), one targeted (specific requirements, recent changes, implementation).

Source priority for reading:

1. **Official government/regulatory bodies** — FDA.gov, europa.eu, AICPA, NIST, state sites. Always check the official source even when an explainer looks comprehensive.
2. **Established law firm guides** — note the firm and date.
3. **Compliance platform explainers** — Vanta, Sprinto, Drata, OneTrust. The most readable "how to comply" guidance, but they are selling a product and are incentivized to make compliance look complex; cross-reference every requirement and timeline against official sources.
4. **Industry association resources.**
5. **Recent enforcement news** — what is actually being enforced.

### Phase 2: Writing

1. **Disclaimer** — MANDATORY, at the very top:

   > **Disclaimer**: This document is informational research only and does not constitute legal, tax, or professional compliance advice. Regulatory requirements are complex, jurisdiction-specific, and change frequently. Consult a qualified attorney or compliance professional before making decisions based on this research.

2. **Summary** — 3-5 bullets: does it apply, key requirements, timeline/cost, what to do first.
3. **Does This Apply to You?** — often the most valuable section. Give the precise scope conditions ("GDPR applies if you process personal data of EU residents, regardless of where your company is based"), not "most companies."
4. **Key Requirements** — table or numbered list grouped by category. Per requirement: what it is in plain language, what you must actually do, deadlines and thresholds.
5. **Process and Timeline** — steps, realistic timeline, estimated cost, common bottlenecks; for ongoing compliance, the recurring obligations.
6. **What's Changed Recently** — updates, new enforcement guidance, pending changes, all dated.
7. **Consequences of Non-Compliance** — fines, real enforcement examples with numbers, business impact ("enterprise customers won't sign without SOC 2").
8. **Next Steps** — what to do first, who to consult.

Label what is legally *required* versus what is merely recommended best practice. Date everything ("as of the January 2026 enforcement guidance") and note where a rule is jurisdiction-dependent (US state privacy laws, EU vs. UK GDPR). Where compliance genuinely needs a professional, say so rather than implying the user can self-serve.

Cite each requirement with a link to the official source: `[FDA 510(k) Submission Process](url)`. For practical guidance, note the source type (law firm, compliance platform, official body). Include a **Sources** section.

### Scope calibration

- **Applicability check** ("Does GDPR apply to us?"): disclaimer first (mandatory, every scope) + Summary + Does This Apply to You? + Next Steps + sources.
- **Standard guide** ("What do we need for SOC 2?"): full structure, emphasis on Key Requirements and Process/Timeline.
- **Deep analysis** (multi-jurisdiction or multi-regulation): expanded requirements, jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction comparison, decision trees.
