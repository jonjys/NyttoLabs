# Deck playbooks — per-type outlines and narrative arcs

Condensed playbooks for the seven common deck types. Each gives the audience, the canonical arc (one slide per beat unless noted), the variants that change the shape, the voice and density defaults, what to cut first, and the failure modes that matter. Layout craft lives in references/deck-design.md; these decide WHAT the slides say.

Read the variant lines before you write the arc: several types have a named variant whose shape is genuinely different from the base arc, and running the base arc on one of those produces a deck aimed at the wrong reader.

## Startup fundraising deck

Audience: investors skimming hundreds of decks for a defensible insight, evidence of demand, and a venture-scale path. 2–3 minutes if you're lucky — every slide must land in a single glance. Target **10–14 slides** as a leave-behind, 5–7 idea-bearing slides live.

Arc: 1) Title — company + one-line description, no tagline gymnastics. 2) Problem — concrete pain, anchored to real people/businesses. 3) Solution — what you do, few words; repeatable one-liner. 4) Traction — numbers that mean something (revenue, retention, growth); write the headline beside the chart ("3× QoQ"). 5) Insight — the non-obvious thing you understand; often the slide that earns the meeting (1–2 slides). 6) Business model — who pays, pricing, unit economics as known. 7) Market — bottoms-up sizing, not a consultant TAM. 8) Team — founders only; specific unfair advantages. 9) Ask — how much, what it buys, where you'll be in 12 months.

Rules: legible (large, bold, high-contrast, key text near the top), simple (one idea per slide — two ideas = zero), obvious (state conclusions on the slide, don't make the reader derive them). Avoid: burying the ask/one-liner past slide 3; essays (hedging reads as weakness); screenshots as explanations (3 bullets beat a screenshot; if you must, crop hard + annotate); diagram mazes; vanity metrics (signups, followers); top-down TAM; decoration.

Stage notes: **Pre-seed / seed** — often no traction yet, and that's fine. Lean harder on the insight and team slides. The implicit pitch is "we understand this market in a way other people don't, and we're the right ones to build this." Slide 4 (traction) becomes "early signals" — letters of intent, design partners, waitlist conversion, founder-led customer interviews. **Series A** — the bar is evidence: real revenue, real retention, real unit economics. Insight matters less; execution matters more. Expect investors to skip directly to the metrics slide. Make it bulletproof.

Voice & density: confident, specific, plainspoken. No buzzwords, no "synergistic," no "revolutionary," no "disrupting." Founders who write the way they talk to a smart friend over coffee win. Density sparse — most slides should have a single headline plus one chart, list, or visual element; if a slide has more than ~25 words of body copy, it almost certainly needs to be split or trimmed. Voice: first-person plural ("we") for the company, active verbs, numbers over adjectives.

Cut first: most decks are too long, not too short. Strong candidates for cutting: detailed product roadmap, exhaustive competitive matrix, advisor logos, "our values" slides, lengthy use-of-funds breakdowns beyond a few categories, and any slide whose primary purpose is to look thorough rather than to advance the narrative. **If a slide isn't earning the next meeting, it's costing it** — the governing test for the cut lists on this page.

## QBR / executive business review

Audience: customer stakeholders (day-to-day + at least one decision-maker) or internal leadership. The deck supports a conversation about the last 90 and next 90 days; 70% of the meeting should be discussion, not narration. Target **8–14 slides** + appendix for raw data.

Arc: 1) Cover/agenda — 3–5 items, confirm live. 2) Executive summary — the 3–5 things that matter, scannable in 30 seconds; the deck's thesis. 3) Performance vs last quarter's commitments — hit/partial/missed, honest, one sentence per miss. 4) Key metrics — the 3–6 KPIs that define THIS relationship, always with comparison (last quarter/year/plan). 5) ROI & business impact (customer) or pipeline & forecast (internal) — the slide the QBR lives or dies on; tie usage to outcomes in the customer's language. 6) What worked / what didn't — short candid retro. 7) Risks, blockers, asks — framed as decisions with owners and dates, not vague requests. 8) Next-quarter plan — goals, target metrics, owners. 9) Action items + next QBR scheduled before the meeting ends.

Rules: headlines state takeaways ("Adoption up 38%"), not sections ("Q2 Metrics"); every number gets a baseline; personalize ruthlessly (a re-logoed template is the fastest path to churn); built to be talked over, not read. Avoid: status-update-in-disguise, vanity metrics, soft-pedaled risks, no-exec-in-the-room, ending on "any questions?".

Variants: **Customer-facing QBR (CSM → customer)** — most common. Demonstrate ROI, refresh the mutual success plan, surface risks, set up the next quarter. The implicit purpose is renewal and expansion — but never pitch directly inside the QBR; let the value do the work. **Executive Business Review (EBR)** — same shape as a QBR but pitched at senior leadership and held annually or semi-annually. Heavier on strategic alignment and longer-term roadmap, lighter on operational detail: cut the tactical metrics and add a "where this partnership is going in 12–24 months" view. **Internal sales QBR** — a rep or region presenting attainment, pipeline, and plan to leadership. Heavy on forecast credibility, deal-by-deal review of top opportunities, coverage math, and a candid "what's at risk"; more frank, more granular, less polished than a customer QBR. **Customer success / portfolio QBR** — a CS leader reviewing a portfolio of accounts to internal leadership; a hybrid of the internal and customer-facing forms, focused on retention, expansion pipeline, at-risk accounts, and resource allocation across segments. **Light-touch / digital QBR** — for long-tail accounts where a live meeting isn't economical. Same arc, delivered as an async deck or recorded walkthrough with a short follow-up call for any actionable items. Lean even harder on personalization to compensate for the missing live conversation.

Voice & density: plainspoken, candid, slightly understated — confidence comes from the data and the honesty around it. Avoid "tremendous," "incredible," "best-in-class"; use specific verbs and specific numbers. Sparse on body slides (single headline + chart, or single number + sentence); higher density on the exec summary, the performance-vs-commitments grid, and the action-items slide where scanning matters. Past tense for the quarter just ended ("Adoption grew…", "We hit…"); present/future for what comes next ("Next quarter we focus on…", "We'll commit to…"); first-person plural for the partnership ("we"), even on internal QBRs where "we" includes the leadership in the room. Attribution is specific: name the team or workstream that drove a result — customers can tell when "the platform delivered X" should have been "your team adopted X faster than the average customer." Internally, the inverse: don't take credit for a regional tailwind.

Cut first: anything that exists to look thorough rather than to drive a decision. Strong cuts: company overview slides, exhaustive feature/release lists (mention only what's relevant to this customer), generic methodology slides, "our values" pages, repetitive metric variations, and any retrospective slide that doesn't end in a concrete change. Push raw data, dashboards, and supporting tables to the appendix. The body of the deck should only contain what the room needs to make the next set of decisions.

## Case study / client results

Audience: a renewing client, an evaluating prospect, or leadership making a scale/sustain/kill call. Sophisticated about results, impatient; missing baselines read as spin. Target **8–12 slides** (a single-slide proof point is a valid compressed form: logo + situation + headline result + one supporting metric).

Arc (STAR variant): 1) Cover — client + one-line outcome headline; the thesis. 2) Situation — who, what they cared about, the BASELINE metrics. 3) Task — the specific outcome promised, in the client's language. 4) Approach — the concrete decisions and trade-offs, with 1–2 real artifacts (campaign structure, roadmap); never "we ran a strategic engagement". 5) Results — one headline metric per slide, each chart paired with a plain-English conclusion, always with comparison. 6) Why it worked — the transferable insight; separates a case study from a brag sheet. 7) What's next / the ask.

Rules: numbers carry every key slide — a 60-pt number with one sentence beats a six-point chart; honest attribution ("our work contributed to…" beats claimed credit); acknowledge one or two dead ends — sanitized case studies read as fiction. Avoid: vanity metrics, cookie-cutter methodology diagrams, selling inside the proof, one giant dashboard slide, closing without a recommendation.

Variants: **Agency / consulting recap** (most common) — built to renew or expand the relationship; heavy on cumulative outcomes, ends with a pitch for the next phase (paid-ads year-end recaps, consulting engagement summaries, program retrospectives). **Marketing case study (external)** — published as a leave-behind, lives on a website, shared with prospects; more polished, anonymized where required, often paired with a customer quote, designed to read well without a presenter. **Internal program review** — lives between QBR and case study: same arc, but the audience is leadership making a fund/scale/kill call, so lean harder on the "why it worked" and "what we'd do differently" beats — those are what justify continued investment. **Single-slide proof point** — a case study compressed into one frame and dropped into a larger sales or capabilities deck: logo + situation + headline metric + one supporting number. Treat it as a complete arc in miniature. **Portfolio / hiring case study** — a different genre (a designer or engineer trying to land a role). Process and decision-making matter more than ROI; most of the guidance above still applies, but emphasize trade-offs, constraints, and what you'd do differently — generic "I ran a design sprint" decks read as bootcamp work.

Voice & density: specific, plainspoken, slightly understated — confidence comes from the numbers; you don't have to amplify them with adjectives. **"We launched X. Conversions tripled." beats "We executed a transformative campaign that delivered exceptional results."** Sparse: most slides should have a single headline + one number / chart / artifact, with body copy under ~25 words wherever possible; the optional "summary of impact" slide can be denser. Past tense for the engagement ("We deployed…", "Conversions reached…"); present/future tense for the recommendation at the end ("Next, we'd…", "This unlocks…"). Attribution honest: use "we," "the team," and "the client" precisely and specify which decisions came from where — false attribution is the fastest way to lose credibility with the people who were actually in the room.

Cut first: almost any slide that exists to look thorough rather than to advance the proof. Strong cuts: agency capabilities slides, team bios, methodology diagrams that aren't engagement-specific, process timelines without dates, exhaustive deliverable lists, generic competitive context, and any "lessons learned" beyond one or two pointed ones. If the slide doesn't make the numbers more credible or the recommendation more obvious, it's filler.

## Client / sponsorship proposal

Audience: a named champion (needs ammunition), a decision-maker (skims for relevance, scope, price), and procurement/legal (scope boundaries, terms). Target **10–15 slides**, ~12 the right ceiling. **Lead with the client's story, not yours** — credentials come near the end.

**A sponsorship or partnership proposal has a different shape — do not run the arc below unchanged.** Lead with mutual fit and audience overlap, not the buyer's pain. Quantify what each side brings; what each side gets. A section on the activation plan replaces "scope of work." The rest of this section — the density split, the exclusions discipline, the real CTA — still applies.

Arc: 1) Cover — client name as a peer to yours; title references THEIR situation. 2) We've done the homework — their market/context; earns permission to continue. 3) The problem — their pain in their own discovery language. 4) Why now — the urgency; without it the proposal sits. 5) The opportunity — quantified upside in outcomes they care about. 6) The idea — ONE big idea, not five frameworks. 7) How it works — phases, deliverables, milestones, executable-plain. 8) Scope of work — bulleted, scannable. 9) What's NOT included — the most underrated slide: exclusions (content production, photography, hosting, third-party costs, maintenance) protect both sides. 10) Team — real names and faces. 11) Why us — credentials through the lens of the brief; one slide. 12) Timeline / first 90 days. 13) Investment — total cost, right categories, currency explicit, payment schedule; tier good/better/best when budget is uncertain. 14) How we'll measure success — exec-recognizable KPIs. 15) Next steps — a real CTA.

Rules: anchor on outcomes, not deliverables; state assumptions (what you need from the client, revision rounds, scope triggers); price stated clearly, never buried. Avoid: credentials-first, generic templates, vague scope language ("ongoing optimization"), hidden caveats, "let me know what you think" closes.

Variants: **Agency / services pitch** — heavier on idea and approach (beats 6–7); credentials and case studies matter more. **Vendor scope (SaaS, dev work, web build)** — heavier on scope, exclusions, and timeline; the idea slide compresses, and the *what* and the *how much* are the deck. **Sponsorship / partnership proposal** — different shape: lead with mutual fit and audience overlap, not the buyer's pain. Quantify what each side brings; what each side gets. Section on activation plan replaces "scope of work." **Internal proposal (campus, sponsor permission, vendor approval)** — treat the approving party as the buyer; lead with what their constituency gets, not what you get. A few beats compress for smaller engagements: a $5K project doesn't need a 90-day plan slide; a sponsorship proposal doesn't need an exclusions slide. Cut to fit, but keep the order.

> Unsure which proposal shape an ask wants? `moda ask "is this a client services proposal or a sponsorship proposal, and which arc should the deck use?"`.

Voice & density: confident, peer-to-peer, plainspoken. Avoid jargon, hedging, and aspirational adjectives — the buyer is an adult; talk to them like one. A small amount of personality on the opening or closing slide ("Genuinely excited to work on this — your category is one we've thought about for a long time") is welcome; chest-beating is not. Density splits front to back: **sparse on idea slides, denser on scope/financial slides. The buyer's exec team will skim the front half and study the back half — format accordingly.** Voice: first-person plural ("we"), present tense for the proposal, future tense for outcomes; use the buyer's name and the buyer's industry vocabulary throughout — it signals you've actually listened.

Cut first: long agency histories, founding stories, or "our values" slides; competitor matrices unrelated to the buyer's specific situation; generic stock photos, decorative iconography, animation flourishes; process diagrams that exist to look thorough rather than to communicate; and open-ended "let's talk" closes — replace with a specific next step. If a slide isn't moving the buyer toward yes, it's moving them toward no. Cut hard.

## Executive strategy deck

Audience: people who control resources; ten minutes, adversarial reading, thousands of these seen. The deck drives a decision, not awareness. Target **10–15 slides** working session, 6–8 board read-out. Structure: **Situation → Complication → Resolution**.

Arc: 1) Cover — names the decision asked for, not the topic. 2) Executive summary — the whole argument on one slide (the one place dense structured text is right); a reader who stops here can still decide correctly. 3) Context — what's true today. 4) Complication — what's breaking, anchored in a concrete fact. 5) Reinforcing evidence (1–3 slides) — MECE-clean angles. 6) The key insight — the non-obvious lever; if it doesn't write itself, the deck has no spine. 7) Strategic options — 2–4 paths scored against the same criteria. 8) Recommendation — one path, conviction; the title IS the recommendation. 9) Roadmap — phases, owners, first 30/60/90 days. 10) Financial impact — conservative/base/aggressive; the CFO probes the weakest number. 11) Risks and mitigations — real ones, as a table. 12) The ask — approval, budget, decision by date X. 13) Appendix — must withstand mid-meeting inspection.

The three habits: **answer first** (pyramid — recommendation, then arguments, then evidence), **action titles** (read the titles top-to-bottom as a paragraph; if they read like a table of contents, restructure), **MECE decomposition**. The 5-second test: a stranger glances at any body slide and can state its takeaway. Avoid: analysis without an argument, options without a recommendation (or vice versa), risks only in the appendix, hockey-stick financials with no bridge, hedged language ("may face headwinds" → "will compress 200 bps without intervention"), frameworks as decoration (a 7S or Five Forces earns its place only if it changes how the reader sees the problem), mixed levels of abstraction ("expand to Asia" next to "renegotiate the printing contract").

Slide patterns: action title + a single chart (most common — the chart fills **60–70%** of the slide, a source line, one or two callouts on the data the title cites) · action title + 3–4 parallel supporting cards · action title + a 2x2 or matrix with the implication annotated · a comparison table with the recommendation emphasized · a prose recommendation slide · a process/architecture diagram, sparingly and only when the system *is* the message. **Source lines on every data slide.**

Visual conventions: restrained palette (two or three brand colors plus one accent), one sans-serif family at two weights, hierarchy by size and weight rather than color, body text readable from the back of a room, generous margins, one idea per slide, **identical footer and section dividers throughout**. **No animation, no transitions, no decorative imagery** — this deck will be screenshotted, printed and forwarded, so every slide must work as a still image. On this surface that rule is live and specific: no entry animations, no timeline motion, no shader background that only resolves in-app, however cheap they are to add.

> Unsure which slide pattern carries a given point? `moda ask "which executive-strategy slide pattern fits a slide comparing two options against the same criteria?"`.

Variants: compress for an internal audience where trust exists (skip options, compress risks). Board and external audiences warrant all thirteen beats.

Cut first: long team/methodology intros (they go to the appendix), industry context the reader already knows, stacked frameworks, charts that fill space rather than support a claim, and "thank you" slides beyond the ask. Density is front-loaded with the answer and back-loaded with the proof — sparse on idea slides, dense on the summary, the business case, and the risks.

## Product / solution deck

Audience: technical evaluators — engineers, architects, IT decision-makers, integrators. Skeptical by default; marketing language burns credibility in seconds. They want depth, integration surface, and evidence of real deployment. Target **10–14 slides** external (15–20 internal launch).

Arc: 1) Cover — product, one-line definition, category positioning. 2) The market reality — the buyer's world today, real numbers. 3) Why existing solutions fall short — 2–4 pointed structural critiques. 4) The product thesis — your answer + 3–4 design principles. 5) Architecture — the most important slide: one clean layered diagram, each layer one sentence, concrete protocols/technologies named; no mystery "AI Engine" boxes. 6) Key capabilities — 4–6 max, each with a one-sentence outcome; capability cards, not feature bullets. 7) Integration/deployment — name the protocols, systems, and deployment modes; vagueness here is a tell. 8) Proof of substance — scale numbers, deployments, references. 9) Why this / why now — one closing stance. 10) Roadmap only if the reader needs it (partners); for evaluations, promises read as weakness.

Rules: big numbers with units and context, restrained single-accent color, real annotated artifacts over illustrations, get to the architecture by slide 5. Avoid: "revolutionary/best-in-class", black-box diagrams, capability theater (12 features that can't survive 60 seconds of Q&A), illegible screenshots, over-claiming AI without saying what kind on what data, checkmark comparison grids.

Type and composition — the genre's aesthetic conventions, in the px ladder a 1920×1080 deck uses (a 16:9 slide is 960×540pt, so **1pt = 2px** at that canvas; the role ladder itself lives in references/design-quality.md): **headlines 36–60pt = 72–120px. Single key numbers can go to 100pt+ = 200px+. Body copy 18–22pt = 36–44px, and sparse. Whitespace everywhere. Stripe / Datadog / Vercel docs energy: clean, premium, product-forward.** Capability cards, not feature bullets — a clean **2x2 or 3x2 grid with icon + name + one-line outcome** reads better than a bulleted feature list, and each card answers "what does this do for me?" not "what is this called?" Real artifacts beat illustrations: a cropped, annotated dashboard screenshot is gold *if it's legible at presentation size* — if you can't show the real thing legibly, show a clean architectural rendering, not stock art. Architecture diagrams are the centerpiece and deserve real time: horizontal layered flow ("connect → standardize → analyze → act") or a boxed system view with clearly labeled interfaces; no mystery boxes, no decorative arrows, no "AI" cloud blob, every label specific. Restrained color: pick one accent and use it surgically — heavy gradients, multiple accent colors, and decorative shapes signal "designed by marketing," which is the wrong audience.

> Unsure what an integration slide has to name to be credible? `moda ask "what protocols, systems and deployment modes should a product deck's integration slide name?"`.

Variants: **External technical evaluation deck** (most common) — what gets sent to an architect or IT decision-maker as part of an RFP or evaluation; tight 10–14 slides, heavy on architecture, integration, and proof, optimized to read well without a presenter. **Internal product launch / enablement deck** — "here's the new platform we built," for an internal team, partners, or sales; can run longer and lean more on the capability tour and roadmap, and the competitive critique slide is often softened or skipped. **Channel / OEM / integrator deck** — for a partner who'll embed your product; shifts emphasis to the integration model, **SDK/API surface, deployment topology, and commercial collaboration framework**. Architecture stays central; the capability tour gets compressed. **Technical kickoff deck** — a first meeting with an enterprise integration team, a research collaborator, or a major customer; shorter (**6–10 slides**), more agenda-shaped: scope, architecture, open questions, next steps. The architecture and integration slides are the spine; everything else exists to frame the working session.

Voice & density: peer-to-peer technical — the implicit voice is "another engineer at the table," confident without bravado, specific without being defensive. Phrases that fit the register: "by design," "out of the box," "open architecture," "infrastructure-agnostic," "deployable in X." Phrases that don't: "transform your business," "best-in-class," "world-class," "trusted by leaders." Density sparse but information-rich — each slide carries one diagram or capability cluster surrounded by whitespace, so the deck looks lean even though every slide has real substance; that's the right shape. Voice: active, third-person product-as-subject ("[Product] ingests data via BACnet…", "The platform routes events to…"); first-person plural ("we") only on the closing slides where positioning gets explicit. Numbers always specific: "Across 300+ buildings" beats "at scale"; "Identifies failures 2–4 weeks early" beats "predicts failures". **Round numbers are fine; round adjectives aren't.**

Cut first: generic "why our category matters" preambles longer than two slides, team bios, TAM and market sizing, pricing and packaging, customer logos without context (use proof slides with real numbers instead), "trusted by leaders" credibility filler, roadmap promises beyond the next concrete release, generic stock photography of people, offices, or "AI brain" graphics, and side-by-side vendor comparison tables — win on substance, not on "✓✓✓✗" grids. If a slide doesn't deepen the reader's understanding of *what the product is and how it works*, it's not earning its place.

## Training / workshop deck

Audience: practitioners who must APPLY the skill afterward; participatory by default. Success = retention and use, not applause. Target **15–35 slides** (1–2 min/slide live) — training prefers more, simpler slides over fewer crowded ones.

Arc: frame → teach → demonstrate → practice → reinforce. 1) Title + learning objectives ("by the end you'll be able to…", 3–5). 2) Why this matters — a real scenario or recognizable mistake. 3) Core concept — the one framework; parts at a glance, then one at a time. 4) Step-by-step walkthrough — one step per slide, 30–90 seconds each, immediately followed by a concrete example; the longest section. 5) Worked example — end-to-end. 6) Common pitfalls — 3–5 anti-patterns with one-line fixes; highest-leverage retention aid. 7) Practice — even a 60-second "try this now" turns listeners into learners. 8) Recap — restate objectives as outcomes; repetition is a feature. 9) Resources / next steps.

Rules: one concept per slide with breathing room; every concept slide gets a buddy example slide; visuals do work (diagrams, annotated screenshots, worked formulas — no stock photos); chunk with section dividers; the deck must pass the read-without-the-presenter test; vary density deliberately (dense mechanics, sparse framing). Avoid: feature-tour tool training (anchor to 3–5 jobs-to-be-done), death by screenshot, pitch creep, skipping the exercise, importing sparse-pitch-deck minimalism wholesale.

Variants: **Internal enablement / sales training** — compresses the framework section and leans hard on examples and pitfalls; the audience already knows the domain and wants the playbook, so use role-play prompts, real customer scenarios, and "what to say when…" slides. **Tool / product onboarding** — anchored to jobs-to-be-done, not menus; screenshots and live walkthroughs do the work, often paired with hands-on exercises in the actual tool. **Workshop (live, with exercises)** — more white space, more discussion prompts, shorter sections; the deck supports the workshop, it doesn't drive it, and **sections should be sized to natural breaks (every 15–20 minutes)**. **Webinar / educational explainer** — shorter (**5–15 slides**), tighter narrative, designed to introduce a concept and hand off to a CTA; often becomes evergreen content, so readability without a presenter is paramount. **Classroom / lecture** — longer arcs, more reference material, often paired with a syllabus; slides survive being studied alone and include sources and citations explicitly. **Practitioner / professional CME-style training** — domain expertise being shared with peers (advisors, clinicians, engineers); tone is collegial, not didactic — **the audience knows a lot already and wants the differentiated insight, not a 101 they could find anywhere. Skip the basics.** **Conference talk / keynote-as-training** — a talk that teaches a method to a room that paid to be there; closer to a webinar than to internal enablement: **1 big idea, 2–3 supporting cases, 1 actionable takeaway.** Resist the temptation to cover everything.

> Unsure which training variant an ask calls for? `moda ask "is this a webinar explainer or a live hands-on workshop deck?"`.

Voice & density: patient, plainspoken, occasionally warm — the presenter is a guide, not a salesperson; specific is always better than impressive, and a worked example beats a clever phrase. Density variable: denser than a pitch deck on mechanics slides, equally sparse on framing slides. Most concept slides should sit around 30–60 words; example and walkthrough slides can hold more if structured. Avoid wall-of-text — if you have a paragraph, it goes in the speaker notes or the workbook, not on the slide. Voice: second person ("you'll be able to…", "now you try…") for participatory framing, then descriptive ("the model uses…", "this step does…") for mechanics; active verbs throughout, no academic passive voice. **Pacing cues on the slide itself are fine — even helpful: "Try this now (60 seconds)", "Discuss with your neighbor", "Pause and reflect".** They earn their space because they change how the audience uses the slide. Calibrate pace by density: a title with three steps in 16pt (≈32px) text and a screenshot underneath is a dense slide that takes 2 minutes to teach; a single sentence in 48pt (≈96px) text is a 20-second beat. Mix both deliberately — long stretches of dense slides exhaust the audience.

Cut first: marketing capabilities slides, presenter bios beyond a one-line credibility marker, exhaustive history of the topic, every menu/feature in a tool, generic stock-photo illustrations, "any questions?" slides used as filler, and any slide that doesn't serve teaching, demonstrating, practicing, or recapping. If a slide doesn't help the learner do the thing afterward, it's costing time the rest of the deck needs. A useful test: imagine the audience two weeks later, trying to remember what they learned — every slide should make their answer to "what was that session about?" sharper, not fuzzier. The slides that fail this test are the ones to cut.
